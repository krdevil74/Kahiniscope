Kahiniscope Production — build and ship guide

From design prototype to a live Play Store app · 14 September 2026

Where the project actually stands

The design is complete and behaves correctly: registration approval, episode-wise segmentation, per-member status, completion percentages, the 7 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 escalation ladder, and the master-admin rule bound to owner@kahiniscope.example. Everything it does, it does in the browser with sample data.

It is not yet an app. Five things are missing, and they are the whole remaining job: a database, sign-in, a server that runs on a schedule, real message delivery, and an Android package. Expect roughly three to five weeks of part-time work, most of it in weeks one and two.

Plan the calendar backwards

If your Play Console account is new, a mandatory 14-day closed test with 12 testers stands between you and a public listing. Open the developer account and start recruiting testers in week one, in parallel with the build — not after it.

The stack I would use

You are one admin and under a dozen contributors. Nothing here needs a server you maintain. Pick the boring option at every layer.

Step 1 — Firebase project and data shape

Create a Firebase project, enable Authentication with the Google provider, and create a Firestore database in the region closest to you (asia-south1 for Bangladesh). Four collections carry everything:

  • users — uid, name, email, phone, telegramChatId, craft, status (pending / approved), role (owner / admin / member), fcmToken
  • episodes — code, title, airDate, status
  • tasks — episodeId, assigneeUid, type, dueDate, done, doneAt, remindersSent, lastReminderAt
  • reminderLog — taskId, channel, sentAt, result. This is what feeds the reminder feed on the board, and what you check when someone claims they never got a message.

Episode completion percentage is not stored. Count done tasks against total tasks for that episode at read time. Same for the slate figure.

Step 2 — Admin by verified email

Anyone can install the app and sign in with Google. What they see afterwards is decided entirely on the server, never in the app UI.

  1. Hard-code the owner address in a Cloud Function, not in the app bundle: const OWNER = "owner@kahiniscope.example"
  2. On first sign-in, a function checks the token. If email === OWNER and email_verified === true, it sets a custom claim role: "owner" and status approved.
  3. Every other sign-in is written as status pending, role member. They get the holding screen until you approve them.
  4. The owner can promote an approved member to admin from the Notify screen. Promotion is itself a Cloud Function that refuses to run unless the caller's claim is owner.

Then write Firestore security rules to match, because the UI is not security. Members may read only tasks where assigneeUid == request.auth.uid and may write only the done field on those. Admins may read and write everything. Pending users may read nothing.

One caution on Gmail as the owner address: if you ever lose that account you lose admin access. Keep two-factor authentication on it and add a second address as a co-owner in the same constant once the app is stable.

Step 3 — The escalation engine

This is the heart of the app and it is about forty lines of code. One scheduled Cloud Function, running once a day at 9am Dhaka time:

GAPS = [7, 4, 3, 2, 1]   // then daily

for each task where done == false:
    step = min(task.remindersSent, 4)
    gap  = GAPS[step]
    since = daysBetween(task.lastReminderAt ?? task.assignedAt, today)
    if since >= gap:
        send(task)                       // channel chain, step 4
        task.remindersSent += 1
        task.lastReminderAt = now
        log(taskId, channel, result)

Two rules keep it from being annoying: skip sends during quiet hours and queue them for 9am, and stop the moment done flips true. Because the gaps are read from a settings document rather than the code, the ladder stays editable from the Notify screen exactly as designed.

Deploy with firebase deploy --only functions. Scheduled functions need the Blaze (pay-as-you-go) plan, but a daily job over a dozen tasks stays inside the free allowance — expect a bill of zero.

Step 4 — Notification channels, in order

Send down a chain and stop at the first success. Log every attempt.

1. Push notification — free, do this first

Firebase Cloud Messaging. Store each device's token on the user record at sign-in, send to it from the function. Unlimited and free. It fails only when the app is uninstalled or the token is stale, which is exactly when you want a fallback.

2. Telegram bot — free and unlimited, easiest to set up

Message @BotFather, run /newbot, keep the token. Each member opens your bot once and presses Start; your webhook captures their chat_id and saves it to their user record. After that, sending is a single POST to api.telegram.org/bot<token>/sendMessage. No approval process, no per-message cost. Add inline buttons and people can mark a task done straight from the chat.

3. WhatsApp Cloud API — where your team already lives

Create a Meta developer app, add the WhatsApp product, register a phone number, and get a permanent system-user token. Business-initiated messages must use a pre-approved template, so submit one like Reminder: {{1}} for {{2}} is {{3}} days overdue. and wait for approval. Budget for setup friction here — number verification and template review are the slow parts. Meta has repeatedly changed its pricing and free-tier terms, so check the current rates for Bangladesh before you rely on it; treat WhatsApp as the nice-to-have and Telegram as the workhorse.

4. SMS — last resort only

Textbelt's free key allows one message per day, which is fine as a genuine last resort for a badly overdue task, and useless as a general channel. If SMS matters to you, a local Bangladeshi gateway bought in bulk will cost less and deliver better than any international free tier. Reserve SMS for tasks that have already passed the daily-reminder stage.

Step 5 — Build the Android app

  1. Scaffold with npx create-expo-app, then port the screens from the prototype. The layouts, spacing and colour logic transfer nearly one-to-one.
  2. Set the app identity: package name such as com.kahiniscope.production, the yellow logo as the launcher icon, and a splash screen.
  3. Wire Google sign-in, Firestore listeners, and FCM. Test on a real phone with eas build --profile development.
  4. Target the API level Play currently requires — it moves every year, and Play Console tells you the number when you upload.
  5. Produce the release bundle: eas build --platform android --profile production. Let Expo manage the signing key, and keep a backup of it.

Step 6 — Publish on Google Play

  1. Open a developer account. One-time 25 USD fee, plus identity verification with a government ID and address. Verification can take a few days, so do it first.
  2. Create the app entry in Play Console: name, default language, free, app not a game.
  3. Fill the store listing. Short and full description, the logo as a 512×512 icon, a 1024×500 feature graphic, and at least two phone screenshots. Screens from the prototype work fine.
  4. Complete the policy forms. Content rating questionnaire, target audience, data safety declaration (you collect names, email, phone numbers — declare them), and a privacy policy at a public URL. A short honest page is enough; Play will reject a missing one.
  5. Run closed testing. Upload the bundle to a closed track and invite testers by email or link. If your personal developer account was created after 13 November 2023, you must keep at least 12 testers opted in continuously for 14 days before you can apply for production access. Recruit 15 to absorb dropouts, and check engagement in Console from day one rather than day thirteen. Organisation accounts and older personal accounts are exempt.
  6. Apply for production access once the 14 days are complete. You answer a short questionnaire about your testing and readiness.
  7. Release. Promote the bundle to production. Review typically takes a few days for a first submission. Use a staged rollout if you want a safety margin.

Your twelve testers can be the production team itself, plus friends. They need real Android devices, real Google accounts, and they have to actually open the app. Emulators and duplicate accounts do not count.

A sensible order of work

Week Do this
1Firebase project, data shape, Google sign-in, the owner-email rule and security rules. Open the Play developer account and begin identity verification.
2Port the screens, wire live data, build the escalation function, connect push and the Telegram bot end to end.
3Store listing, privacy policy, data safety forms. Upload to closed testing and start the 14-day clock with 15 testers.
4–5Run one real episode through the app while testing runs. Add WhatsApp templates. Apply for production access and release.

One shortcut worth considering: for the first month, skip the Play Store entirely. Deploy the same screens as a web app on a free host, have the team add it to their home screen, and drive reminders through the Telegram bot. You would find out within one episode whether the escalation rhythm is right, and the two weeks of closed testing would then be spent on a design you had already proved.
